Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of cost-efficient scheduling of multiple scientific workflows in cloud environments under deadline constraints. The objective
is to design a workflow brokerage mechanism that coordinates the execution of
concurrently arriving workflows while minimizing cost and maintaining reliable
deadline satisfaction. To this end, we propose a hybrid resource provisioning and
scheduling framework that combines reserved and on-demand cloud resources.
Reserved resources are prioritized to exploit their lower cost, while on-demand
instances are dynamically provisioned when the workload exceeds the reserved
capacity. The brokerage mechanism integrates a static planning phase, which
constructs an initial execution plan for each workflow based on conservative
execution-time estimates and schedules tasks at their latest feasible start times,
with a dynamic scheduling phase that jointly manages ready tasks from multiple
workflows at runtime according to system state and resource availability. This
coordinated strategy improves resource utilization while preserving capacity for
future workflow arrivals. Extensive simulation experiments using real scientific
workflow benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed approach reduces execution cost compared to on-demand-based methods and achieves higher deadline
satisfaction rates than approaches relying on unreliable low-cost resources.
Keywords: Cloud computing, multiple workflow scheduling, workflow as a service,
reserved instances, cloud brokerage

1 Introduction
Scientific workflows provide a fundamental abstraction for structuring, automating, and executing complex scientific and data-intensive applications across a broad
range of domains, including biology, astronomy, and agricultural sciences. Modern
workflows increasingly incorporate Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI)–driven workloads—such as large-scale data preprocessing, model training,
hyperparameter optimization, and inference pipelines—which significantly increase
both computational demands and orchestration complexity [1]. These workflows typically consist of hundreds or thousands of heterogeneous, precedence-constrained tasks
with diverse resource requirements (e.g., CPUs, GPUs, and high-memory nodes) and
complex data dependencies.
Cloud computing provides a particularly suitable execution platform for such
workflows due to its elasticity, on-demand resource provisioning, and support for
specialized accelerators, enabling dynamic scaling and cost-aware resource management under budget constraints [2]. Consequently, cloud platforms have emerged as a
key enabler for executing next-generation HPC workflows that integrate traditional
scientific computing with large-scale data-driven and AI-oriented processing.
Executing such large-scale applications on cloud infrastructures remains a significant challenge. It requires careful provisioning of appropriate resources, as well
as efficient scheduling and mapping of workflow tasks onto those resources while
respecting precedence constraints. These decisions must satisfy users’ Quality of Service (QoS) requirements—such as deadlines, reliability, and security—while keeping
resource rental costs within acceptable limits. Although prior studies have explored
user-side schedulers for single workflows [3–5], such approaches are limited in their
ability to handle more complex and dynamic scenarios. This challenge has led to
the emergence of Workflow-as-a-Service (WaaS) brokers [6]. Acting as intermediaries
between Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) and users, WaaS brokers select and lease
suitable cloud services for workflow execution while explicitly considering user-defined
QoS parameters. In addition, they help alleviate vendor lock-in by enabling workflows
to be executed across multiple CSPs, thereby reducing dependence on a single provider
and mitigating the risk of monopolistic practices.
In realistic WaaS environments, workflows arrive at the broker in an online and
unpredictable manner, without prior knowledge of future submissions. For each incoming workflow, the broker must dynamically provision appropriate cloud resources and
schedule tasks while respecting precedence constraints and users’ QoS requirements.
At the same time, it seeks to minimize resource rental costs by provisioning resources
only when needed and by selecting cost-effective service and pricing options. This
online execution setting requires the broker to carefully balance immediate scheduling
decisions with long-term cost efficiency.
To attract users, WaaS brokers aim to reduce workflow execution costs compared to
direct user-managed execution while simultaneously maximizing their own profit. They
primarily achieve this through two strategies. First, brokers execute multiple workflows
from different users concurrently in order to increase the utilization of leased cloud
resources, thereby amortizing costs across workflows. This leads to the Multiple Workflow Scheduling Problem (MWSP), which is inherently more complex than scheduling
2

a single workflow due to increased contention and coordination requirements [7]. Second, brokers exploit the diversity of cloud service models and pricing options—such
as Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Container as a Service (CaaS), and Function as
a Service (FaaS), combined with on-demand, spot, and reserved pricing—to improve
efficiency and reduce execution cost.
Most existing research on the MWSP focuses on the traditional IaaS model with ondemand pricing [8–13], while a separate line of work explores container-based execution
models under on-demand pricing [14–16]. These studies largely overlook the use of
more cost-efficient pricing options, such as spot and reserved instances, which have
the potential to significantly reduce workflow execution costs when incorporated into
scheduling and resource provisioning strategies. Spot instances, in particular, provide
access to excess cloud capacity at significantly reduced prices but introduce substantial
uncertainty, as they may be revoked by the cloud service provider with minimal notice
[17]. To mitigate this risk, some approaches combine on-demand resources for critical
tasks—where interruptions could violate users’ QoS constraints—with spot instances
for less critical tasks [18–22]. Although such hybrid strategies often reduce cost, the
inherent unreliability of spot resources can still jeopardize QoS guarantees.
Given these limitations, reserved pricing models offer a more stable alternative for
reducing execution cost. In this model, cloud resources are leased for an extended duration—typically one year or longer—at a substantial discount compared to on-demand
instances. However, excessive reliance on reserved resources may lead to underutilization, thereby increasing operational costs for the WaaS broker. A practical strategy
is therefore to provision an appropriate baseline of reserved instances that achieves
high utilization, while using on-demand instances to accommodate peak workloads.
Since reserved instances are prepaid, the broker should prioritize their usage and maximize their utilization through careful task scheduling. However, allocating reserved
resources too aggressively may prevent future workflows with tighter deadlines from
meeting their timing constraints, whereas excessive reliance on on-demand resources
can significantly increase operational cost.
Despite its clear economic and operational advantages, the joint use of reserved and
on-demand instances for workflow scheduling under QoS constraints remains largely
unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has addressed this problem in
its general form [6]. Existing studies are limited to specific cases, such as the scheduling of periodic workflows with predefined submission intervals [23] or linear pipelines
rather than general workflows [24]. Consequently, a comprehensive scheduling framework that jointly exploits reserved and on-demand resources for general workflows is
still missing.
To bridge this research gap, we propose Cost-efficient cloud Broker for executing
Multiple scientific Workflows (CBMW), a cost-efficient cloud brokerage mechanism
for executing multiple workflows using a hybrid resource provisioning model that
combines reserved and on-demand instances. The broker prioritizes the utilization
of reserved resources to exploit their lower cost, while dynamically provisioning ondemand instances when the workload exceeds the reserved capacity. This strategy
enables the system to achieve both cost efficiency and reliable deadline satisfaction.

3

To support this provisioning model, CBMW employs a hybrid scheduling strategy
that integrates static planning with dynamic scheduling. Upon the arrival of a workflow, a static planner constructs an initial execution plan before execution begins.
Using the workflow deadline as a constraint, the planner attempts to assign each
task to reserved instances at the latest feasible start time that still satisfies the overall workflow deadline. This conservative placement preserves reserved capacity for
future workflows that may arrive with tighter timing requirements. During execution,
a dynamic scheduler manages the set of ready tasks according to the current system
state and resource availability. While the static plan provides a guideline, the scheduler may adapt task placements or start times when better opportunities arise, such
as when reserved instances become idle earlier than expected.
The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

 We propose a cloud brokerage mechanism for WaaS environments that efficiently
integrates reserved instances and on-demand containers for the execution of multiple
workflows.
 We design a hybrid scheduling strategy that combines static deadline-aware planning
with dynamic runtime scheduling, enabling both cost-efficient resource allocation
and adaptive execution.
 We introduce a conservative reserved-resource placement strategy that schedules
tasks at their latest feasible start times to preserve capacity for future workflows.
 We conduct extensive simulation-based evaluations using real scientific workflow
benchmarks to analyze the performance of the proposed approach under different
workload intensities and deadline constraints.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in

mechanism and its hybrid scheduling strategy. Section 5 evaluates the performance of
the proposed approach through simulation experiments. Finally, Section 6 concludes
the paper and outlines directions for future work.

2 Related Work
In this section, we review prior studies on the design of WaaS brokers in cloud
environments. Since the primary responsibilities of a WaaS broker are resource provisioning and task scheduling, we also review studies addressing the MWSP. The
reviewed studies are categorized based on the pricing models adopted for renting cloud
resources: on-demand resources, a combination of spot and on-demand resources, and
a combination of reserved and on-demand resources.

2.1 On-demand Resources
The on-demand pricing model is the most widely adopted model for scheduling and
executing multiple workflows in cloud environments. Consequently, a large body of
prior work has focused on MWSP under this model, with most approaches relying on
heuristic, meta-heuristic, or learning-based techniques.
4

Several studies employ heuristic algorithms to address cost minimization under
workflow deadline constraints. Liu et al. [8] proposed the onliNe multi-workflOw
Scheduling Framework (NOSF), which uses a greedy heuristic to minimize execution cost while satisfying deadlines. In this framework, each task is scheduled on
the resource that leads to the minimum cost increase while meeting its assigned
sub-deadline. To handle uncertainty in task execution times, execution durations are
modeled as random variables following a normal distribution, and this uncertainty is
incorporated into scheduling parameters such as earliest start time. Similarly, Chen
et al. [9] proposed the unceRtainty-aware Online Scheduling Algorithm (ROSA) to
solve the same problem. ROSA adopts a comparable greedy scheduling strategy while
explicitly considering execution time uncertainty. Task execution times are modeled
using normal distributions, and α-quantiles are used to estimate predicted start and
finish times for workflow tasks. These predicted values guide the selection of the most
appropriate VM for task execution. Experimental results show that ROSA achieves
cost reductions of up to 56% compared to five competing algorithms.
In a related direction, Ye et al. [10] proposed a stochastic multi-workflow dynamic
scheduling algorithm (SMWDSA) that explicitly models both task execution time and
workflow arrival as stochastic processes. The approach decomposes workflow deadlines
into task-level sub-deadlines and schedules tasks based on minimum available time
slots on heterogeneous VMs to reduce cost while satisfying deadline constraints. In
addition, a feedback mechanism dynamically updates task priorities and sub-deadlines
during execution to mitigate the impact of uncertainty and improve overall scheduling efficiency. Taghinezhad-Niar et al. [25] also proposed two heuristic algorithms for
scheduling deadline-constrained workflows in a WaaS broker using containers under
execution-time uncertainty. One algorithm minimizes cost, while the other targets
energy consumption. Their approach distributes the workflow deadline into task-level
sub-deadlines and prioritizes ready tasks using the Earliest Deadline First (EDF)
heuristic, assigning them to available resources accordingly.
Meta-heuristic algorithms have also been extensively investigated for MWSP under
the on-demand pricing model. Li et al. [11] applied Particle Swarm Optimization
(PSO) to jointly optimize workflow makespan and execution cost. To improve the
exploration of the solution space, they proposed a Multiswarm Co-evolution (MSC)
strategy that consists of three swarms: one swarm optimizing cost, one optimizing
makespan, and a third optimizing a normalized objective combining both metrics.
These swarms cooperate to generate candidate solutions, which are further refined
using a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to explore elite individuals and improve convergence.
To address the same optimization objectives, Li et al. [26] proposed a co-evolutionary
algorithm based on the Poor and Rich Optimization (PRO) meta-heuristic. Their
approach maintains four populations: the first two populations independently optimize
makespan and cost, respectively; the third population serves as a global information
exchange pool containing all non-dominated solutions from the first two populations;
and the fourth population maintains an elite solution set composed of the best individuals selected according to a predefined threshold. The proposed algorithm achieves
a performance improvement of up to 25.62% compared to state-of-the-art methods.

5

In addition to heuristic and meta-heuristic methods, reinforcement learning (RL)
has been increasingly adopted to tackle MWSP under on-demand pricing. Li et al. [12]
proposed a Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN)-based framework to jointly minimize
workflow makespan and execution cost. Their method employs a two-level scheduling
architecture: at the first level, a pointer network selects one task from the set of ready
tasks—defined as tasks whose parent tasks have completed execution—and at the second level, the selected task is assigned to a VM by the DDQN agent. Pan and Wei
[27] also employed a Deep Q-Network (DQN) to schedule multiple workflows, with
the objective of minimizing makespan while maximizing overall resource utilization.
Furthermore, Zhang et al. [28] proposed a hybrid approach combining Genetic Algorithms and DQN to schedule multiple workflows with the joint objective of minimizing
makespan and execution cost.
Although these studies demonstrate the effectiveness of on-demand-based scheduling approaches, they rely exclusively on the highest-cost pricing model offered by cloud
providers. As a result, they are inherently limited in their ability to reduce execution costs, as they do not exploit lower-priced alternatives such as spot or reserved
instances.

2.2 Hybrid Spot and On-demand Resources
To further reduce execution costs, several studies have explored the use of low-cost
but unreliable spot instances in combination with on-demand resources. In these
approaches, on-demand instances are typically used to guarantee deadline satisfaction,
while spot instances are leveraged opportunistically when sufficient slack is available.
Taghavi et al. [22] proposed the Cost-Efficient Workflow-as-a-Service Broker
(CEWB), which classifies cloud resources into multiple reliability-based classes. Ondemand instances constitute the highest reliability class, while spot instances are
assigned to lower classes based on their maximum bid price. Workflow tasks are
also classified according to their criticality, which is determined by their slack time.
Tasks are then mapped to appropriate resource classes such that highly critical tasks
are scheduled on more reliable resources, whereas less critical tasks are executed on
cheaper spot instances. Zolfaghari et al. [18] extended this model to the scheduling of
workflow ensembles, defined as sets of workflows with similar structures but different
sizes. Similarly, Chen et al. [21] proposed a heuristic algorithm for scheduling workflow ensembles on hybrid spot and on-demand resources, aiming to reduce execution
cost while meeting deadlines.
Other researchers have explored hybrid spot–on-demand scheduling using heuristic
strategies. Zhou et al. [20] proposed Dyna, which first derives an execution plan based
solely on on-demand instances using the A* algorithm to minimize cost while satisfying
deadline constraints. The initial plan is subsequently refined by selectively migrating
tasks to spot instances when sufficient deadline slack is available.
More recently, Lin et al. [19] proposed SptDAG, a reinforcement learning–based
framework for scheduling workflows on hybrid spot and on-demand resources. Their
6

approach employs a deep neural network to select among a set of predefined metapolicies, including prioritizing the use of spot instances, launching new spot or ondemand instances, assigning tasks to existing idle instances, or deferring task execution
to a subsequent scheduling period.
Overall, while hybrid spot and on-demand approaches are effective in reducing
execution costs, their dependence on unreliable spot instances can jeopardize servicelevel agreements, particularly for workflows with strict deadline requirements.

2.3 Hybrid Reserved and On-demand Resources
The combination of reserved and on-demand instances offers a compelling pricing
model for workflow scheduling, as it combines the reliability and discounted cost of
reserved instances with the elasticity of on-demand resources. Despite its practical
importance, this model has received limited attention in the literature, especially for
general workflow scheduling scenarios.
Taheri et al. [23] focused on scheduling periodic workflows under a hybrid
reserved–on-demand pricing model, where workflow submissions occur at predefined
intervals with fixed patterns. Their approach follows a static planning–dynamic
scheduling paradigm. In the static planning phase, the problem is formulated as a
resource leveling problem (RLP) to derive an initial schedule that maximizes the utilization of reserved resources. In the dynamic scheduling phase, tasks are executed
according to the static plan, while on-demand instances are provisioned when reserved
capacity is insufficient. Due to execution-time uncertainty, the static plan may be
adjusted dynamically. However, this work does not address general online workflows
with arbitrary and unpredictable arrival times.
Zhu et al. [24] investigated the scheduling of workflows structured as linear pipelines
on hybrid reserved and on-demand instances. To account for uncertainty in task execution and data transfer times, these parameters are modeled using fuzzy numbers. A
fuzzy task scheduling algorithm is then applied to prioritize ready tasks and schedule
them on reserved instances whenever possible. If no suitable reserved instance is available, the algorithm falls back to existing or newly provisioned on-demand instances
to meet task sub-deadlines. Nevertheless, this study is limited to linear workflows and
does not consider general workflows modeled as Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs).
Considering the existing literature, it is evident that current approaches addressing
hybrid reserved and on-demand resources are restricted to specific workflow structures
or predictable submission patterns. To the best of our knowledge, no previous work
has proposed a WaaS broker capable of cost-efficiently scheduling general DAG-based
workflows with arbitrary arrival times under a hybrid reserved/on-demand pricing
model. To fill this gap, we propose the CBMW.

3.1 System Architecture
Figure 1 illustrates the architecture of the proposed WaaS broker. Users submit workflows to the broker at arbitrary times, each accompanied by a predefined deadline. The
broker is responsible for leasing resources from the CSP and executing workflow tasks
on those resources. To support workflow execution, the broker provisions resources
using both on-demand and reserved pricing models. Reserved resources are leased
as virtual machines (VMs), selected from a single, large instance type—for example,
an HPC-optimized Amazon instance such as hpc7a.96xlarge [29], which provides 192
physical cores and 768 GiB of memory. Workflow tasks are executed on these VMs
using a container management service that runs tasks as containers. In contrast, ondemand resources are provisioned through a service such as AWS Fargate [30], which
enables the broker to directly lease containers without managing the underlying virtual
machines. The specific container configurations are selected according to the resource
requirements of individual tasks.
As illustrated in Figure 1, the broker is composed of four main components: the
Negotiation Module, the Static Planning Module, the Dynamic Scheduling Module,
and the Provisioning Module. The Negotiation Module receives workflows from users
and first verifies the feasibility of the user-defined deadline. If the deadline is acceptable, the module negotiates the execution price according to the system’s pricing
8

Fig. 1: The proposed architecture

policy. To reduce the broker’s execution costs, it actively encourages users by offering lower prices for workflows with flexible deadlines—providing the scheduler greater
freedom to allocate reserved resources efficiently—or for workflows submitted during
off-peak periods when reserved resources are available and underutilized. To determine
the proposed price, the module relies on the static planner to estimate execution costs
based on the current system load and the workflow’s deadline. If the user accepts the
offered price, the workflow proceeds to execution.
The WaaS broker employs a hybrid scheduling approach that combines static planning with dynamic scheduling. Upon the arrival of a workflow, the static planner
constructs an initial execution plan before execution begins. Using the workflow’s deadline as a constraint, it attempts to assign each task to reserved instances at the latest
feasible start time that still satisfies the overall deadline. This conservative placement
strategy preserves reserved capacity for future workflows that may arrive with tighter
timing requirements. When no suitable reserved instance can be identified, the task is
tentatively assigned to an on-demand container. This preliminary plan is subsequently
used by the pricing policy to estimate how much of the workflow can be executed on
reserved resources versus on-demand containers, and to derive an expected execution
cost that forms the basis for the price offered to the user.
At runtime, task execution is governed by the dynamic scheduler. It continuously
monitors the set of ready tasks —those whose dependencies have completed—and dispatches them to available resources based on the current system state and resource
load. While the dynamic scheduler takes the static plan as a guideline, it is not bound
by it and may adjust task start times or resource assignments in response to changing
conditions. For instance, if reserved instances become idle, the scheduler may advance
9

the execution of tasks that were originally planned to run later. Finally, the Provisioning Module manages the lifecycle of on-demand containers by provisioning and
releasing them as requested by the dynamic scheduler.

time represents a conservative bound such that the task execution time is less than
or equal to this value with α% probability. We chose α ≥ 0.9 to provide a stronger
probabilistic guarantee for meeting task deadlines.
To enable data transfer between tasks, the broker employs a shared storage service such as Amazon S3. In this model, a parent task writes its output data to the
shared storage, from which child tasks retrieve the required inputs asynchronously
when needed. This mechanism allows data transfer delays to be estimated in advance;
therefore, the associated data access time is incorporated into the execution time of
each task.

a container management service that launches task containers on VMs. We assume
a short provisioning delay for launching a container on a reserved resource, which is
already considered in the execution time of each task. If a resource rk is allocated to
task tji , we denote it by arij = rk , and astji and af tji indicates the actual start time

The negotiation module is also responsible for determining the execution price

the next subsection) to estimate the raw execution cost of the workflow. This estimate
is computed by multiplying the execution time of each task by the per-second price of

multiplied by a parameter γ , which represents a markup factor capturing the broker’s
operational costs and profit margin, in order to determine the final price quoted to the
user. Workflows submitted during off-peak periods or with relatively loose deadlines
have a higher likelihood of being executed on reserved instances, resulting in lower
raw execution costs and consequently lower prices. The computed price is presented
to the user, and if the user accepts the offer, the workflow is admitted to the system
for execution.

The TaskPlanner algorithm is presented in Algorithm 2. Scheduling a task on a set

Therefore, we propose a backward variant of the sweep-line algorithm that computes
the latest feasible start time for each task.

4.3 Dynamic Scheduling Module
The dynamic scheduler is responsible for executing workflow tasks at runtime, i.e.,
determining the actual start time astji for each task tji . Although the static plan
serves as an initial roadmap for task execution, the dynamic scheduler may adjust the
scheduled start time of tasks for two main reasons.
First, the non-deterministic nature of cloud environments introduces uncertainties
in resource availability and task execution times that must be handled at runtime.
However, because the static planner conservatively uses the α-quantiles of task execution times when constructing the schedule, we expect that many tasks will finish
earlier than predicted.
Second, while the static planner schedules tasks at their latest feasible start time
in order to preserve reserved resources for future workflow arrivals, at runtime some
reserved resources may become idle earlier than expected. In such situations, the
dynamic scheduler may execute tasks earlier than planned to improve the utilization
of reserved resources.
The dynamic scheduler adopts a periodic strategy that divides time into short
intervals. During each interval, newly available ready tasks are collected, and scheduling decisions are made at the end of the interval. A task becomes ready when the
execution of all its predecessor tasks has completed. The scheduler maintains a list of
ready tasks sorted in ascending order of their scheduled start time.

The provisioning module acts as the execution interface between the broker’s scheduling decisions and the underlying cloud infrastructure. Its primary responsibility is
to provision containers and execute tasks on the selected resources. For reserved
instances, the module relies on a container management service to launch containers
on VMs. In contrast, when tasks are assigned to on-demand resources, a serverless
container service such as AWS Fargate provided by Amazon Web Services is used to
directly provision containers without requiring explicit management of the underlying
VMs.
In addition to initiating task execution, the provisioning module also manages task
completion events. When a task finishes, the module releases the allocated container,
updates the resource state by removing the corresponding events from the assigned
reserved resource (if applicable), and inserts the task’s ready successors into the list
of ready tasks maintained by the dynamic scheduler.

5 Experiments
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed brokerage system, we conducted a
comparative analysis using simulation. In the following subsections, we describe the
experimental setup and analyze the results obtained from the simulations.

5.1 Experimental Setup
We utilized CloudSim [33] to simulate the cloud environment and the execution of
workflows. The reserved resources are modeled as 50 instances of AWS hpc7a.96xlarge
[29], each providing 192 physical CPU cores and 768 GiB of memory. On-demand
resources are modeled as AWS Fargate Linux/X86 containers [30], whose resource
specifications match the requirements of the tasks executed on them.
The workflow traces are generated using five real-world scientific workflows, namely
Montage, CyberShake, LIGO, Epigenomics, and SIPHT [34]. These workflows are
produced using the Pegasus Workflow Generator with sizes ranging from 50 to 1000
tasks in order to ensure both application and size diversity. The runtime provided
by Pegasus for each task is considered as its expected execution time µ, while the
standard deviation is set to σ = 0.1µ to model runtime variability.
Since no previous work addresses workflow scheduling using a combination of
reserved and on-demand instances, we compare our approach with two representative
cloud workflow brokers. The first is NOSF [8], which operates solely on on-demand
resources. The second is CEWB [22], which employs a combination of spot and ondemand instances. To ensure a fair comparison, identical assumptions are applied
across all algorithms, including second-level billing granularity. The primary evaluation metric is the total monetary cost, while the deadline success rate and resource
utilization are considered as secondary metrics.
To evaluate the performance of the competing algorithms under diverse conditions,
we consider three workload scenarios. In each scenario, workflow arrivals are modeled
as a Poisson process with different arrival rates λ. Specifically, we define: light load

5.2.4 Overall discussion
Overall, the experimental results demonstrate clear trade-offs among the evaluated
brokerage strategies. Algorithms that rely heavily on on-demand or spot instances
benefit from high elasticity and potentially lower monetary cost, but they may suffer
from higher provisioning latency or resource interruptions.
CBMW adopts a hybrid strategy centered around reserved resources, which
enables stable execution and predictable performance. Under moderate and heavy load

regimes, CBMW achieves a favorable balance between cost efficiency and reliability
by maximizing the utilization of its reserved capacity before resorting to on-demand
provisioning.
In contrast, CEWB consistently achieves the lowest cost due to its aggressive use
of spot instances, but its reliability decreases in scenarios with tight deadlines or high
workload intensity. NOSF, which relies solely on on-demand instances, provides stable
execution but incurs higher costs because it cannot benefit from the discounted pricing
of reserved or spot resources.
These observations indicate that CBMW is particularly suitable for workflow-based
applications where deadline satisfaction and execution reliability are critical, while still
maintaining competitive cost efficiency compared with purely on-demand solutions.

6 Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we presented CBMW, a cloud brokerage mechanism for executing multiple scientific workflows in WaaS environments. The proposed approach combines
a hybrid resource provisioning model with a hybrid scheduling strategy. Specifically,
CBMW integrates reserved and on-demand instances to balance cost efficiency and
execution flexibility. Reserved resources are prioritized to exploit their lower cost,
while on-demand instances are dynamically provisioned when the workload exceeds
the reserved capacity.
To effectively utilize these heterogeneous resources, CBMW employs a hybrid
scheduling mechanism that integrates static planning and dynamic runtime scheduling. The static planner constructs an initial deadline-aware execution plan that assigns
tasks to reserved resources at their latest feasible start times, while tasks that cannot
be accommodated are tentatively assigned to on-demand containers. During execution, a dynamic scheduler manages ready tasks according to the current system state
and adapts scheduling decisions when resource availability changes.
Extensive simulation experiments using several real scientific workflow benchmarks
demonstrate that CBMW achieves a favorable trade-off between cost and deadline
satisfaction. By maximizing the utilization of reserved resources while maintaining the
elasticity of on-demand provisioning, the proposed approach significantly reduces execution cost compared with purely on-demand brokerage strategies while maintaining
high deadline success rates.
Future work will focus on improving the resource provisioning strategy of the
proposed brokerage framework. One promising direction is to develop predictive mechanisms for estimating the optimal amount of reserved capacity based on historical
workload patterns and expected workflow arrival rates. Such predictive provisioning
could help determine an appropriate baseline of reserved instances that maximizes
utilization while minimizing unnecessary reservation costs. Another direction is to
extend the brokerage model by incorporating spot instances in addition to reserved
and on-demand resources. While spot instances offer significant cost reductions, their
interruption-prone nature introduces reliability challenges. Therefore, future research
will investigate reliability-aware scheduling strategies that selectively utilize spot
22

resources without compromising workflow deadline guarantees or overall execution
stability.
